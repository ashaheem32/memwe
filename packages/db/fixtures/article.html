Why Postgres Row Locks Are Enough

By Ada Example · 4 March 2026 · 6 min read

Every queue library eventually grows a scheduler, a lease table, and a reaper. This post argues that for most teams a single SELECT ... FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED does the job, and that the operational simplicity is worth more than the throughput you give up.

The argument has three parts:
what a lease actually protects,
where the throughput goes, and when to move on.

What a lease protects

A lease is a promise that one worker owns a job until a deadline. The failure it guards against is a worker that dies mid-job. With row locks the database keeps that promise for you: the lock disappears with the connection.

  • No heartbeat table to keep in sync
  • No clock skew between workers
    • Each worker trusts the database clock
    • Which is the only clock that matters for ordering
  • No reaper to write, test, and page on

Where the throughput goes

The classic objection is contention. Here is the claim schedule we measured on a 4-core box:

WorkersClaims / sp99 latency
11,9003 ms
87,40011 ms
328,10040 ms

The query that produced those numbers is short:

update jobs set status = 'running', locked_by = $1
 where id = (
   select id from jobs
    where status = 'pending' and run_at <= now()
    order by run_at
    for update skip locked
    limit 1)
returning *;

"Skip locked" is the whole trick. Everything else is bookkeeping.

When to move on

  1. When a single database cannot hold the queue's write rate
  2. When jobs must be delivered to another region
  3. When you need fan-out to thousands of consumers

Until one of those is true, the row lock is enough. It has been enough for us at ten thousand jobs a minute for two years.
